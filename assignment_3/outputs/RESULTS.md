## 2. The two algorithms (task 2)

Both tokenizers share the same whitespace + punctuation pre-tokenizer, are
trained only on the training split, and mark word-internal pieces with `##`,
so every number below is directly comparable.

| | BPE | WordPiece |
|:--|:--|:--|
| textbook training criterion | merge the most **frequent** adjacent pair, `count(ab)` | merge the best **likelihood gain**, `count(ab) / (count(a) x count(b))` |
| how a word is segmented | replay the learned merge list, in order | scan greedily, take the **longest** vocabulary piece that matches |
| unseen word | falls back to characters | emits `[UNK]` if no piece matches |

> **A caveat that the measurements forced into the open.** Section 6 finds the
> two vocabularies are ~100% identical, which is not what the table above would
> predict. The cause is the library, not the language: huggingface's
> `WordPieceTrainer.train()` does not implement the likelihood criterion at all -
> it trains an ordinary frequency-based BPE model and converts it with
> `WordPiece::from_bpe()` (`tokenizers/src/models/wordpiece/trainer.rs`). So the
> **left column of row 1 applies to both runs**, and what this report actually
> compares is row 2: the same inventory applied by two different segmentation
> algorithms. That is still a real and measurable difference - see section 6 -
> but it is not the training-criterion comparison the textbook describes.

|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|

Fertility barely moves. That is a real result, not a missing effect: once the

|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| BPE | 100,000 | 74.6% | 72.8% |
| BPE | 300,000 | 85.6% | 84.5% |
| BPE | 500,000 | 89.8% | 87.7% |
| BPE | 1,000,000 | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| WordPiece | 100,000 | 74.6% | 72.2% |

| WordPiece | 500,000 | 89.8% | 86.9% |
| WordPiece | 1,000,000 | 100.0% | 100.0% |

So training size changes *which* subwords are learned much more than it

## 6. BPE against WordPiece

Both trained on the same 1,000,000 sentences.

| vocab size | shared vocabulary | test sentences tokenized identically |
|--:|--:|--:|
| 20,000 | 100.00% | 62.8% |
| 30,000 | 100.00% | 72.6% |
| 50,000 | 99.98% | 80.8% |

The two vocabularies are **99.98% identical** at worst, yet the
tokenizers agree on only 80.8% of test sentences or fewer.

The identical vocabularies are not a fact about Hindi. Chasing this number
down leads to the library: huggingface's `WordPieceTrainer` does not
implement WordPiece's likelihood criterion - `train()` builds a normal
frequency-based BPE model and converts it with `WordPiece::from_bpe()`
(`tokenizers/src/models/wordpiece/trainer.rs`). Both runs therefore select
their vocabulary with the *same* algorithm, so ~100% overlap is the
expected outcome and anything else would be the surprise.

What the disagreement column measures is real, though, and it is the
cleanest result here: **holding the vocabulary fixed, the choice of
segmentation algorithm alone changes how 19.2% or more of sentences are tokenized.** BPE replays its
ordered merge list; WordPiece greedily takes the longest matching piece.
Same pieces, different segmentation - as `प्र ##भा ##वी` against
`प्रभाव ##ी` in section 7 shows.

A true BPE-vs-WordPiece *training* comparison would need a trainer that
actually scores pairs by likelihood gain; no widely used library ships one.

## 8. What the numbers show

* **BPE, more data:** going from 100,000 to 1,000,000 training sentences at vocab 30,000 moves test fertility from 1.071 to 1.068 (-0.003). Fertility saturates, but that model shares only 74.6% of its vocabulary with the 1,000,000-sentence one and tokenizes 27.2% of test sentences differently.
* **WordPiece, more data:** going from 100,000 to 1,000,000 training sentences at vocab 30,000 moves test fertility from 1.069 to 1.066 (-0.003). Fertility saturates, but that model shares only 74.6% of its vocabulary with the 1,000,000-sentence one and tokenizes 27.8% of test sentences differently.
* **BPE, bigger vocabulary:** 20,000 -> 50,000 types at 1,000,000 sentences moves test fertility from 1.105 to 1.041 (-0.064), and words kept whole from 93.0% to 97.1%.
* **WordPiece, bigger vocabulary:** 20,000 -> 50,000 types at 1,000,000 sentences moves test fertility from 1.102 to 1.039 (-0.063), and words kept whole from 93.0% to 97.1%.
* **BPE vs WordPiece:** averaged over every completed configuration, BPE produces 1.072 tokens per word and WordPiece 1.070. They share 99.98% of their vocabulary at 50,000 types - because huggingface trains both vocabularies with the same frequency-based BPE trainer (section 2) - yet still tokenize 19.2% of test sentences differently, purely from merge-replay vs longest-match segmentation.
* **Unknowns:** neither algorithm emitted a single [UNK] on dev or test, in any configuration. Both learn their alphabet from the training split, and 100k Hindi sentences already contain every character the held-out sets use, so every unseen word can still be spelled out from pieces.
